A Beginner's Guide to Investing

10 October 2026

Ways to hold investments

The same fund can sit inside different types of account, called wrappers. The wrapper doesn't change what you invest in; it changes the tax you pay and when you can get your money out. Figures below are for the 2026/27 tax year (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027).

The main trade off

ISAs give flexibility: tax free money you can reach whenever you need it. Pensions give bigger tax breaks, as the government adds tax relief on the way in, but the money is locked away until your late 50s. Many people use both: a pension for retirement, and an ISA for goals in between.

Worth knowing

  • Free money first: if your employer matches pension contributions, paying in enough to get the full match is usually the best return available anywhere.

  • Salary sacrifice: some employers let you swap part of your salary for pension contributions, which also saves National Insurance.

  • Use it or lose it: unused ISA allowances don't roll over to the next tax year. Pensions can carry forward unused allowance from the previous three years in some cases.

  • Cash ISA change: from 6 April 2027, under 65s will only be able to put £12,000 a year into a cash ISA. The remaining £8,000 can still go into stocks and shares ISAs.

  • Tax outside a wrapper: in a general investment account, dividend tax is 10.75% for basic rate and 35.75% for higher rate taxpayers above the £500 allowance.

Employee share schemes

Many large employers offer their own schemes, alongside the wrappers above.

  • Sharesave (SAYE): you save a fixed amount each month for three or five years, then choose whether to buy company shares at a discounted price fixed at the start, or simply take your cash back. You can't lose your savings, only the chance of a gain.

  • Share Incentive Plan (SIP): shares bought from your salary before tax, sometimes with free matching shares. They're tax free if kept in the plan for five years.

  • The risk: holding lots of your employer's shares means your job and your savings depend on the same company. Many people sell and spread the money into a fund over time.

Sources: Bestinvest: tax allowances 2026/27; EQ Investors: tax rates and allowances (Lifetime ISA limit).

The building blocks

Everything you invest in is either a slice of a company (shares) or a loan to someone (bonds). Funds just bundle lots of them together.

Where currency comes in

You rarely buy currency directly in a pension or ISA. Instead it affects you behind the scenes. If your fund owns US companies, it holds investments priced in dollars. When the pound strengthens against the dollar, those holdings are worth slightly less in pounds, and vice versa. This is called currency risk. Over decades it tends to even out, and owning investments in many currencies is part of being well spread.

Tracker funds vs active funds

A tracker (or index fund) simply copies a market, such as the 1,000 or so largest companies worldwide. It is cheap because nobody is picking stocks. An active fund has a manager choosing what to buy, aiming to beat the market. It costs more, and most active funds fail to beat a cheap tracker over the long run.

Low, medium and high risk funds

Risk mostly comes down to one thing: how much of the fund is in shares versus bonds and cash. More shares means bigger ups and downs, but historically more growth over long periods.

How to choose

  • Time is the biggest factor. The longer you can leave the money, the more risk you can usually afford, because there is time to recover from falls. A Junior SIPP can't be touched for 50+ years, so it can generally take more risk than savings for a holiday.

  • Check the fund's risk score. Every fund's Key Information Document shows a number from 1 (lowest) to 7 (highest). Most platforms, including AJ Bell, display it on the fund page.

  • Be honest about how you'd react. If seeing a 30% drop would make you sell, a lower risk fund you stick with is better than a higher risk one you abandon at the worst moment.

  • Spread it out. One global fund holding thousands of companies is far safer than a handful of individual shares.

Accumulation (Acc) vs Income (Inc)

For growing money over the long term, choose Acc. Most funds come in both versions: same investments, same charges, and the only difference is what happens to the dividends and interest the fund earns.

Accumulation (Acc)Income (Inc)
What happens to dividendsAutomatically reinvested inside the fundPaid out to you as cash
EffectYour number of units stays the same, but each unit's price grows fasterYou get regular cash, but the unit price grows more slowly
Best forBuilding wealth, pensions, Junior SIPPs and ISAs you won't touch for yearsPeople who want a regular income, such as retirees
How to spot it"Acc" in the fund name, e.g. Fidelity Index World P Acc"Inc" or "Dist" (distributing) in the name

Reinvesting means your dividends earn their own returns, which then earn returns too. This snowball effect, called compounding, is what makes long term investing so powerful.

If you pick an Income fund by mistake, the cash just sits in your account uninvested. Some platforms can reinvest it for you, but it's simpler to choose Acc in the first place.

Why small percentages matter

Charges are taken every year from the whole pot, so they compound just like returns do. As a simple illustration, £10,000 left for 30 years with 5% growth before charges would reach roughly:

  • £43,000 with no charges

  • £39,000 with 0.39% charges

  • £28,000 with 1.5% charges

The difference between a cheap tracker and an expensive fund can be a third of your final pot. This is why low cost trackers are popular for long term saving.

Where in the world your money goes

Which countries a fund invests in matters, because every market has its own risks. No single country wins forever, so spreading your money across many is the simplest protection.

Developed vs emerging markets

There's no "undeveloped" category in investing. Index providers like MSCI sort countries into three groups based on how wealthy the economy is and how open, regulated and easy to trade their stock markets are.

GroupWhat it meansExamplesMain risks
Developed (23 countries)Wealthy economies with large, well regulated stock markets and strong legal protection for investorsUS, UK, Japan, Germany, France, Canada, AustraliaNormal market ups and downs; some markets are expensive
Emerging (24 countries)Fast growing economies whose markets are less matureChina, India, Taiwan, Brazil, South AfricaPolitical upheaval, weaker company rules, government interference, sharp currency swings
FrontierSmaller, early stage markets that are harder to tradeVietnam, Kenya, NigeriaAll the emerging market risks, plus being hard to sell quickly

What about returns? Since 1900, developed markets have returned 8.5% a year on average versus 6.9% for emerging markets. But from 1960 to 2025 the order flipped: emerging markets returned 10.9% a year against 9.6% for developed. Emerging markets can grow faster, but the ride is bumpier and the extra reward isn't guaranteed.

Country by country

The US now makes up 62% of the value of every stock market in the world combined, up from a fairly even spread in 1900. In a global index today, the next biggest countries are tiny by comparison.

A useful quirk: many big UK companies earn most of their money abroad, so the FTSE 100 often tracks the world economy more than the UK's.

The main country risks

  • Concentration: if one country or sector dominates your fund, a bad decade there hits you hard. Japan's stock market peaked in 1989 and took over 30 years to fully recover.

  • Currency: returns from overseas shares go up or down as the pound moves. On average, currency swings have added around 6 percentage points to the overall risk of an investment.

  • Political and policy: elections, trade wars, tax changes or a government seizing control of companies, the last being a bigger risk in some emerging markets.

  • Economic: recessions, inflation and interest rate rises. Since 1900, most big market falls in peacetime were caused by economic problems rather than political events.

No region wins every year

Leadership rotates. In 2024 the developed world index (mostly US) rose 17% while markets outside the US rose just 3%. In 2025 it reversed, with markets outside the US up 29% against 19.5% for the developed world index (both in dollars, before dividends). Nobody reliably predicts these switches, which is why owning a bit of everything works.

How the three funds below compare

The Vanguard LifeStrategy funds put about 21% of their shares in the UK, around seven times the UK's 3% share of the world market. That deliberately reduces US exposure to about 53%, but it is a bet on the UK. Fidelity Index World follows the market as it is, so it holds about 72% in the US and nothing in emerging markets. Neither is wrong; they are different choices about where to take risk.

Three real funds compared

The biggest driver of risk is how much each fund holds in shares. Here are three real funds, from low to high risk, and where your money actually goes.

In all three, the share portion leans heavily towards the US, because US companies make up most of the world's stock market by value.

Low risk: Vanguard LifeStrategy 20% Equity

What's inside: 80% bonds and 20% shares, spread across about ten Vanguard tracker funds. Charge: 0.20% a year.

  • Bonds (80%): two global bond funds make up about two thirds of the whole fund (33.5% and 32.5%). The rest is UK gilts (7.5%), UK company bonds (2.9%) and inflation linked gilts (2.9%).

  • Shares (20%): mostly a developed world tracker excluding the UK (14.3%), plus UK shares (4.1%) and emerging markets (1.6%).

Where the risk lies: mainly in interest rates. When rates rise, existing bonds fall in value, which is why low risk funds like this fell noticeably in 2022. Inflation is the other risk, as it can quietly outpace the returns. The global bonds are hedged back to pounds, so currency swings have little effect.

Medium risk: Vanguard LifeStrategy 60% Equity

What's inside: 60% shares and 40% bonds. Charge: 0.20% a year.

  • Shares (60%): a developed world tracker excluding the UK (32.8%), UK shares (12.6%), a US tracker (7.6%), emerging markets (4.8%) and small amounts in Europe, Japan and the Pacific.

  • Bonds (40%): a global bond fund (32.9%), UK gilts (3.7%), UK company bonds (1.5%) and inflation linked gilts (1.4%).

Where the risk lies: split between both. The shares bring stock market risk, with about half in North America and about a fifth in the UK, a deliberate UK tilt. The bonds cushion falls but bring interest rate risk. Technology is the largest sector at around 25% of the shares.

High risk: Fidelity Index World

What's inside: 100% shares in about 1,280 large and medium companies across developed countries, tracking the MSCI World index. Charge: 0.12% a year. No bonds and no emerging markets.

  • By country: about 72% in the US, with the rest spread across Japan, the UK, Europe, Canada and Australia.

  • By company: the top ten holdings make up 27% of the fund, led by NVIDIA (5.6%), Apple (4.9%) and Microsoft (3.8%), all US technology firms.

  • By sector: technology is the biggest at about 31%, then financials at 16%.

Where the risk lies: almost entirely in share prices, with nothing to cushion falls. It is also concentrated: a bad patch for US markets, US technology companies or the dollar would hit this fund hardest. In return, it has the highest growth potential of the three over long periods.

Sources: LifeStrategy 20%, LifeStrategy 60% (Vanguard factsheets, July 2026, via Ticker); Fidelity Index World portfolio (31 August 2026); HL fund update (US share, April 2025).
