IT Consultancy – Lecture Notes, Blocks One and Two

Module overview

The module treats IT consultancy as management consultancy applied to IT artefacts, and runs in three blocks ending with your group presentation. Block One, Lecture 1: Introduction to the module and IS in the organisation

Core message: IT on its own is not strategic; value comes from how it is introduced into a particular organisation, and that is where consultants should earn their fee.

Why consultancy is ethically contested

  • Consultancy is defined as objective advice, but that objectivity is often questionable. Advice may suit the consultant's revenue rather than the client's need.
  • Sofiane linked the dot-com crash to consultants pushing ideas too hard, and saw a similar cycle in today's AI push. IT tends to reinvent itself every few years, which generates revenue.
  • Test for any tool, AI included: does it actually improve the business? It is easy to adopt something because it impresses, without checking whether it makes a difference.

Two kinds of research in consultancy

  1. Subject-area research. Clients hire consultants for expertise. In this module that expertise must come from critical reading of academic literature, not from what you already know.
  2. Research methods for collecting data inside the client. Every organisation is unique (people, processes, culture, targets), so a consultant must research the organisation before intervening. Sofiane warned against consultants who say on day one they will be there "six to nine months": that assumes the client is the same as the last one.
  • Choose the method only after the aim and objectives are clear. Sofiane's analogy: you decide where you are going before choosing the bus.
  • Questionnaires are often chosen because they are easy to build and distribute, not because they fit the question.
  • In natural science, reliability sits in replicable data. In social science (business schools), results rarely generalise, so reliability sits in the methodology. "Tell me how you collected the data and I'll trust the process."
  • Data collection can also validate a solution, for example usability testing or stress testing a new system.

Module themes

  • Enterprise systems. Still the biggest problem area after 30–40 years; Sofiane put properly assessed success rates at around 20–30%. Many SMEs still handle data as they did in the 1980s.
  • The "garden shed" principle of data. Storing data is easy and cheap; retrieving it is very hard. A new, unfamiliar query can take days to answer.
  • Paper as the main integration protocol. Where systems are not connected, people copy and re-key data between them (orders, ERP, shipping). "Introduce humans, you're looking for trouble."
  • Outsourcing and IT–business alignment. Sofiane described alignment as the consultant's main duty, and noted consultants often leave at go-live, which is when problems start.
  • Ethics and personal aspects, including upselling and consultants who are hard to remove.

IS as a socio-technical entity

  • Once a system enters an organisation it acquires social dimensions, and these increasingly decide success. Treat system introduction as planned organisational change.
  • Training is not enough. People forget most of a short training session, and practising in a relaxed setting is not the same as real use. Sofiane argued for "education": psychological preparation that explains why the change is happening.
  • Involve end users in decisions. People who feel part of the decision are much less likely to reject the system. System rejection is the worst outcome of an implementation.
  • Psychological contract. Staff hold unwritten expectations (for example that experience earns promotion). A new system can break these: an expert on the old system becomes a novice again, which breeds resentment.
  • People naturally resist change; a system branded "useless" early is very hard to rescue.

What an information system is

  • O'Brien (2004): any organised combination of people, hardware, software, communications networks and data resources that collects, transforms and disseminates information.
  • Information systems are not new; technology is just one way to materialise a solution. Design the solution first, then find technology that fits it.
  • Large organisations now buy packaged software and re-engineer processes to fit it (BPR). You cannot re-engineer well without understanding the system's structure.
  • Data redundancy across functions means "the same" data differs between departments. Sofiane's plumbing analogy: every tap in the house should give the same water.
  • Data, information, knowledge. Data are raw facts with no meaning; contextualised data become information; accumulated information becomes knowledge (much of it tacit). Knowledge is needed to interpret information, and almost everything in organisations is decision-making.
  • Retail stock that shows on the handheld but is not in the stock room, and supermarket wastage, are examples of data out of sync with physical reality.
  • Silos are political as well as technical: functions guard "their" data even when systems allow integration.

Components of an IS

Data, people, hardware, software, procedures, media.

  • Hardware and software are merging as IT shifts from product to service (cloud, service-oriented architecture, the "Lego block" analogy of decoupled services).
  • Data is the fuel; a brilliant system with poor data is useless.
  • Media covers usability: a functional but unusable system is useless.
  • Procedures: systems now dictate processes rather than mimic them. Sofiane saw this reversal behind many current problems.
  • People are the most important component.
  • Organisations that buy a system for each problem over decades end up with a portfolio that is hard to integrate, and must fix it while running business as usual.
  • Their problems are usually information systems problems, not IT problems. "If you have an IT project that's being led by an IT person, expect trouble." Treat it as a business initiative.
  • The alignment trap (introduced here): aligning systems to each user group at micro level, without stepping back to the macro view, creates a worse overall picture.

Evolution of IS

  1. Data processing era: digitising data for efficient storage and transactions.
  2. Management information systems era: using information to solve problems and support decisions, which exposed the need to integrate processes and led to ERP.
  3. Strategic information systems era: does IT contribute to competitive advantage?
  • Many organisations call digitalisation "digital transformation". The transformation is in the process; the digital part only enables it.
  • ERP systems are efficient but not always effective: they remove the informal shortcuts staff use (such as starting shipping before a sale completes), which can slow work down.

Is IT strategic?

  • A resource is strategic if it contributes to and sustains competitive advantage. Competitive advantage rests on a winning, unique strategy (not only profit; Amazon was a leader before it made money).
  • Porter's generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation, focus. Sofiane's critique: Porter's five forces assume organisations are alike and differ only in how they handle external forces.
  • SWOT is two pairs: opportunities and threats (external), strengths and weaknesses (internal). Too much internal focus causes blindness, as with Nokia.
  • IT's contributions: efficiency (with the IT productivity paradox), product, service, image and innovation differentiation. Some firms buy systems mainly to project a modern image.
  • Sustainable competitive advantage needs resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable (resource-based view). D'Aveni et al. (2010) argue it is now rare and short-lived; firms chain temporary advantages instead.
  • IT is valuable but not rare: it is easy to copy (American Airlines' Sabre system is the classic example). Nvidia or Microsoft are different because they produce IT; Barclays uses IT available to everyone (Microsoft, Salesforce, Workday).
  • Carr (2003), "IT Doesn't Matter": owning the tool creates no value; what you do with it does. Sofiane rates it highly.
  • So IT cannot be strategic in absolute terms, but it can drive strategic initiatives.

  • Consultants can help at each barrier: tailoring the implementation, building client capability (unless they deliberately keep clients dependent, which is unethical), and spotting opportunities.
  • Separate advice from implementation where possible. A firm that advises and then implements will steer you to its own solution.
  • Blackpool ERP anecdote. A food manufacturer's Microsoft Dynamics data migration became data cleansing: decades of recipes in complex spreadsheets with multiple versions, needing former chefs to trace them. A fixed SME budget of about £30,000 grew to £40,000–£50,000. Asking "where is your data and can you trace it?" on day one was the consultant's duty.
  • Whether a consultant should push for market leadership depends on the brief, but flagging foreseeable problems with benefit realisation is always the consultant's duty.

Block One, Lecture 2: Introduction to IT consultancy and the consulting lifecycle

Core message: consultancy follows a lifecycle (pre-sales, sales, research, delivery, exit), and almost every engagement problem traces back to something that was never agreed. Richard taught this session.

What management consultancy is

  • O'Mahoney and Markham (2013, p. 11): creating value for organisations by applying knowledge, techniques and assets to improve performance, through objective advice and/or implementing solutions. Richard asked whether consultants are ever truly objective; long-term sector specialists carry prior knowledge of competitors.
  • Reasons clients engage consultants: a check-up, motivation to change, performance standards, handling conflicts (mergers), mending deficiencies. In practice they overlap (the class gave AI competition, complaints, and a Windows migration as examples).
  • Three steps: identify the problem, research and recommend a solution, help implement it. Beware clients who have already chosen the solution.
  • Boundaries of consulting (O'Mahoney and Markham, 2013, p. 12): "pure" management consulting sits in the middle but overlaps with IT, financial and outsourcing consulting and engineering. Be clear which hat you wear (advising, building, operating); each carries different risks.

In-house versus external

  • Neither option is free. In-house looks cheaper because costs hide in salaries and in taking staff off their normal roles. External looks expensive because the fee is visible, but finding, vetting, negotiating with and policing consultants also costs.
  • Transaction cost economics (Oliver Williamson): consider frequency (repeated work makes fees add up), asset specificity (specialised assets), and uncertainty and measurement difficulty (higher uncertainty favours in-house).
  • Examples: a one-off ERP rollout needing rare skills suits consultants; running a bespoke data platform for a year may suit an internal team. Work can be brought back in-house once the knowledge is absorbed.
  • Consultants bring an outside view and knowledge of competitors, but they also learn about you, and it is hard to write a tight contract defining "done". The class noted senior consultants move between firms and in-house roles, and relationships drive repeat work.

IT consulting

  • One of the fastest-growing forms of consulting, driven by new technologies (analytics, cloud, now agentic AI and soon quantum), integration of existing technologies, and governance and regulation (data protection, cyber security, emerging AI rules). Richard thought the regulatory driver is under-rated.
  • IT consulting should be driven by clear business requirements; otherwise clients get over-complicated products, excessive costs and unmanageable projects.
  • Richard stressed the word duty: the consultant must tell the client that equal effort goes into business processes, not only the system. A process fix may earn a smaller fee than a system sale, which is itself a tension.
  • A class point: success is a joint responsibility. Clients who outsource without clear requirements set consultants up to fail.
  • Upselling is common: proposals often end with "the next phase would look like this and cost this". Vendor-led consultancies (the IBM example) can steer every solution to their own platform.

The consulting lifecycle

  • Phases: pre-sales, sales, research, delivery, exit, with tools and methods used throughout. It is not fixed; big firms each have their own version and real engagements are iterative.
  • Research is "often subject to bias": consultants research with an eye on what the client hopes to hear and what they can sell next.

Pre-sales

  • Thought leadership: client-facing insight that builds an image of expertise, often benchmarking and industry reports. Read consultancy material as partly marketing.
  • Service and product development: packaging senior consultants' experience into repeatable, sellable methods. Branded methods (Azure, Salesforce) reassure clients and reduce perceived delivery risk.

Ball Inc. case (in-class reading)

  • An external consultancy trained Ball's staff in lean; the team became so good that Ball hired them out to others; then the team wanted a pay rise, clients poached them, and the original consultancy claimed breach.
  • Lessons: problems trace back to things never agreed (IP, scope of use, client terms, staff terms). The work skipped straight to delivery with no proposal, contract or exit plan. Knowledge transfer has consequences. Ethics pulls in several directions (would you take the offer as one of the five?).

Sales

  • Consulting is a push industry: consultancies help clients perceive a need. Sales is a process, a managed pipeline and a long-game relationship, not a single pitch.
  • A sales strategy forces honesty about what is distinctive (core product, USP), whether the firm can deliver at scale, and who buys and how.
  • Contact routes: cold calling, networking, insiders, up-selling, referrals. Many bids fail; some get a foot in the door for later. Up-selling can slide from spotting a real need to selling what the client does not need. A student cited Covid PPE contracts; When McKinsey Comes to Town was recommended.
  • The proposal turns a conversation into a commitment; it should translate easily into a contract. Know when not to write one (weak client commitment).
  • Contract covers obligations, deliverables, reporting, fees, expenses, IP and liability.
  • Payment models decide who carries risk: fixed fee (consultancy absorbs overruns), time and materials (client does), risk–reward (aligns incentives but only if results can be measured fairly), free consultancy (a loss leader, ethically questionable). Penalty clauses exist because promises are cheap.

Research (covered fully in Lecture 3)

  • Research answers the client's question well enough to act on, not knowledge for its own sake. Flow: problem statement, research questions, methods, data collection, analysis, presentation; each step depends on the one before.
  • Aim, objectives, outcomes: developed early, keep focus, track progress, and are communicated through the proposal.
  • Aim: one sentence, about 10 words at most in Richard's view; broad enough to capture purpose, specific to the firm. An aim that could apply to any company is not specific enough. Example: "It is the aim of this consulting project to develop a promotional plan for the effective launch of the XYZ product range."
  • Objectives: break down the aim; SMART; linked to methods, deliverables, timing and cost; usually around three or four. Test: could someone verify at the end whether you did it?
  • Outcomes: what becomes possible if objectives are achieved: "As a result of this project the business will be able to…". Outcomes sell the project. In process consulting, where you build the client's own capability, the value is real but easy to overlook, so spell it out.

Delivery

  • A genuine partnership is the ideal but hard where one side sells and the other pays. Trust is built by conduct (honesty, transparency, keeping commitments) and by fitting how the client works.
  • Planning trades off scope, speed and economy; you cannot maximise all three, so state your choices.
  • Communication is where projects often go wrong; design it into the plan so the client never has to chase you.

  • Resourcing: name dependencies and treat them as risks. "Silo warfare" happens when people stay loyal to their department; a data owner who won't release data or a team too busy to test can stall a project.

Exit

  • Projects never end cleanly. Plan the handover: build client capability, identify key client individuals, hand over documentation and software, give a contact for follow-up.
  • A good exit leaves the client able to carry on without you. Most plans stop at go-live; say how the phase after it is managed.
  • Withdrawal: consultants embedded for months become "part of the furniture"; leaving is disorientating for both sides.
  • Project review: essential for payment by results, useful for all. Build an evaluation point into the plan and say how success will be judged.
  • Follow-up work: always sought, but a serious ethical distraction.

The proposal structure

No fixed format, but it should contain:

  1. Executive summary (no costs, so the client is not distracted)
  2. Background and rationale (shows you understand the problem)
  3. Objectives and deliverables (do not commit to what you cannot do)
  4. Scope and dependencies (do not leave things open)
  5. Outline approach (name methodologies)
  6. Success measures (realistic KPIs)
  7. Work breakdown and plan (activities, timing, options)
  8. Resources and responsibilities
  9. Cost breakdown, ideally per deliverable
  10. Processes for control, escalation and review
  11. Dependencies and risks
  12. Legal disclaimer (confidentiality)

Stakeholder map, final report, case studies

  • Stakeholder map: who is affected and how much influence each has.
  • Final consulting report: executive summary, introduction (context, aim, objectives, outcomes), body, recommendations (call to action), appendices (evidence).
  • Case studies of past projects reinforce thought leadership and serve as proof of expertise.
  • Conclusion: ambiguity leads to distrust and conflict; document everything for accountability.

Block One, Lecture 3: Consultancy research

Core message: the rigour of your methodology is often the only way to validate consultancy data, so the research aim must dictate the method, and the method must be justified rather than simply declared.

The research element

  • Consultants solve problems clients cannot solve themselves, by collating, interpreting and presenting data to answer business questions.
  • Flow: problem statement → research questions → research methods → data collection → data analysis → data presentation.

Problem statement, locus of control and locus of knowledge

  • Clarify and simplify the client's issue. Ask what the client believes the problem is and why they are focusing on it. Focus on facts, not opinions.
  • Clients' problem statements are often biased, especially about causes. Whoever raises the alarm describes the problem through their own understanding.
  • Locus of control (who is in charge) and locus of knowledge (who understands what is happening) are often different people. Senior leaders usually convey the problem to the consultant, while the knowledge sits with teams on the ground.
  • Where control sits shapes IT investment. In cost-oriented organisations finance leads, and the first question is always "how much?". Local authorities on 12-month budgets favour predictable per-user cloud costs. Innovation-led firms ask what the investment will add.
  • Pitch accordingly: do not lead with the "flashy side" to a cost-focused client.
  • The worst mistake is taking the client's words as the correct version of the problem. Filter the noise and find the root cause before solving anything (Six Sigma is popular because it targets root causes).
  • A student's example: a product with weak adoption; leadership did not know what was happening on the ground, so Red Hat ran a two-day assessment with value stream mapping. This was a gap between locus of control and locus of knowledge.
  • Consultants add their own bias, steering clients towards the solution they sell. Project leads also push their own area first (finance leads want the finance module of an ERP done first).

Research questions or hypotheses

  • Exploratory (inductive) research: you don't know what is going on, so you ask research questions ("Which factors have the most impact on the weak uptake of the enterprise system?").
  • Explanatory (deductive) research: you think you know, so you state testable, falsifiable hypotheses and confirm or refute them.
  • The two run as cycles, one feeding the other. A hypothesis that is not confirmed triggers an exploratory cycle to find out why; a theory built from exploration can then be tested on a larger sample. Sofiane's view: you cannot explore and explain at the same time.
  • Choose deductive when the problem is well defined; inductive when the situation is new to you.

Bias

  • Bias cannot be eliminated (Sofiane called the grounded-theory "blank slate" a myth); the aim is to reduce it.
  • Triangulation: use several methods (for example focus group, then interviews, then observation) and check whether they point to the same result.
  • In interviews, bias grows as you go: earlier interviewees shape what you expect from later ones.

Qualitative and quantitative data

QuantitativeQualitative
Meaning fromNumbersWords
DataNumerical, standardisedNon-standardised, needs categorising
AnalysisDiagrams, statistics (regression, forecasting)Conceptualisation, thematic analysis

  • They are complementary, usually used in sequence (build a theory qualitatively, then validate it quantitatively).
  • Sofiane thinks the strict link between quantitative and deductive is "far too rigid": hypotheses can be tested with interview data, and exploration can use transactional data. What matters is staying within one philosophy (exploring or explaining) at a time.
  • Testing needs a representative sample; large samples are easier with quantitative data. Sampling techniques can be mentioned but need not be detailed in the assignment.
  • MMU tends towards interpretivist research; Manchester Business School is more positivist.

Data collection methods

  • Secondary (documentary) data: meeting minutes, historical records, and the literature review.
  • Literature review: gives background and context, and is how you acquire subject expertise. Use web or practitioner sources only for factual information such as statistics, never as theoretical foundation. Referencing acknowledges sources.
  • Interviews: the most popular method; good for feelings and meanings, including body language. Act as an independent researcher. Structured, semi-structured or unstructured.
    • Open questions let interviewees define and describe a situation ("Why did the organisation introduce this particular enterprise system?").
    • Probing questions request a focus, seek an explanation or reflect a statement back ("Why don't you think that the employees understand the need for this system?"). Revisiting an early question later in a long interview can test consistency.
    • Specific and closed questions open a topic or obtain precise information ("Has the old Central Region been merged with the Southern Region?"); they also give comparable answers across interviewees.
  • Focus groups: the researcher facilitates; group interaction is the point. Useful early on to locate issues before interviewing individuals.
  • Questionnaires and surveys: suit questions about an aggregate population; need a representative sample whose results are extrapolated. Good for numerical data (Likert scales); poor for exploration because people dislike writing answers.
  • Other methods: ethnography (immersion over time, avoiding the snapshot bias of interviews), work shadowing or action research (doing the job yourself), work observation (timing tasks to find bottlenecks, as in lean), think-aloud usability testing.

Food Company X case

  • A corporate survey showed female staff reported much less "dignity and respect" than male staff. The consultant agreed exploratory questions with the client, re-examined the survey (the gap existed only in production, where men outnumbered women three to one), searched other surveys, interviewed workers, interviewed HR, and spent a day working in each area.
  • Root cause: a gendered job-allocation policy put women in tightly controlled packing roles and men in autonomous mixing roles. The HR manager was trained, the practice dropped and job rotation offered; a year later the gap had gone.
  • Sofiane's point: it is easy to draw conclusions early from what the client tells you. Widen the scope and filter the client's noise before judging.
  • Class critiques: training does not change mindsets; a focus group could have shown the group dynamics; recruitment wording and absence data could also be checked; letting staff choose roles may improve acceptance.

Data analysis

  • The analysis should follow logically from the research purpose and apply a framework (statistical methods, thematic analysis).
  • Inductive: observation → pattern → hypothesis → theory. Deductive: theory → hypothesis → observation → confirmation.
  • NVivo is free to MMU students: code interview transcripts to themes and see who said what. Useful for the synoptic project.

Critical writing advice given in this lecture

  • Critical writing means building arguments on several academic sources at once (synthesis). Citing one source at a time is description, even if correctly referenced.
  • Read sequentially, then stop and write "cross-sectionally". Do not write while you read.
  • You don't need to invent counter-arguments for an appropriate choice, though reporting genuinely conflicting views is healthy.
  • Stop reading at theoretical saturation: when you are no longer learning anything new.

Block Two, Lecture 4: IT–business alignment

Core message: alignment is a dynamic capability that matures over time, and it fails mostly for social, political and governance reasons rather than technical ones. Richard taught this session online, using Menti polls and a Padlet board (linked on Moodle) that holds the slides, core papers, case studies and recordings.

Opening poll: alignment at Barclays

The cohort described Barclays' alignment as historically unaligned, complex, fragmented, siloed and slow. Recurring themes:

  • Legacy systems with things "bolted on" over time; challenger banks (Monzo, Revolut) move faster.
  • "New shiny things": initiatives launched then left unfinished.
  • Quarterly planning that favours tactical fixes over long-term strategy.
  • Business stakeholders telling tech to "just make it happen", and tech involved too late ("thrown over the fence").
  • Annual business-case funding that discourages continual reprioritisation. Funding was ranked the top challenge, though one student called it "the excuse".
  • Most respondents saw IT strategy as following business strategy; about 13% saw co-evolution.

Frameworks covered

  • Boundaries of consulting (recap): pure management consulting overlaps with IT, finance, outsourcing and engineering.
  • Two-layer view of IT consultancy: the primary task is balancing business and IT strategy; secondary decisions such as sourcing and enterprise systems sit beneath and can reinforce or undermine alignment.
  • IS strategy triangle: business strategy, organisational strategy and information strategy must align. The consultant diagnoses misalignment across all three rather than treating an IT decision in isolation.
  • External environment: business strategy integrates environmental analysis, internal strategic intent and stakeholder pressures; these set the constraints IS and IT must align with.
  • Intended, deliberate, emergent and realised strategy (Mintzberg): some intended strategy is dropped, new elements emerge from the environment (technology, regulation), and the result is the realised strategy. Consultancy advice must allow for uncertainty and drift rather than assume a fixed plan.
  • Vertical alignment: business strategy (where and how we compete) → IS strategy (what information is needed) → IT strategy (how it is delivered), with feedback at each level. Richard was checking the source of this slide with Sofiane.
  • Strategic alignment model (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993): strategic fit (external) and functional integration (internal) across business strategy, IT strategy, organisational infrastructure and IT infrastructure. Use it diagnostically: failures come from weak links between domains.
  • Dominant alignment perspectives: influence can run business-led, IT-enabled or technology-driven. Misalignment often reflects the wrong planning logic rather than poor execution; identify which perspective the client is in.

A student example: Barclays' in-app name change used Onfido for document checks, but AI-generated fake documents (not in scope three years ago) exposed gaps between internal IT strategy and external partners.

Defining alignment

  • Organisations are defined by the relations between their parts, not just the sum of them. Alignment means applying IT at an appropriate time, in harmony with business strategy.
  • Enablers and inhibitors (from the alignment research table on the slides): the main factors are leadership, governance and relationships, not technology.
  • Intellectual dimension: formal tools, frameworks and plans that test strategic complementarity and structural fit. Necessary but insufficient; alignment on paper can fail in practice.
  • Social dimension: shared understanding, leadership commitment and trust between business and IT executives. This explains why alignment succeeds or fails.
  • Rationale: aligned firms are argued to outperform others, and alignment makes IT investment follow strategic intent.
  • Counter-argument: tight alignment can stifle innovation (no business case yet for emerging tech) and stop IT challenging strategy. Treat alignment as a dynamic, contested process, not an end state.
  • Challenges: ambiguous or unknown business strategy, lack of shared understanding, managers acting within their own locus of comprehension and control, change outpacing formal planning. Students gave examples of cloud and infrastructure migrations that were obsolete by the time they landed.

The alignment trap case

  • Organisations that push to align IT with strategy before IT itself works well end up with a poorly performing IT function aimed at the right goals but failing to deliver: duplicate applications, shadow IT, painful integration, legacy dependent on retiring staff, strategic projects that miss time and budget, and most capacity spent keeping the lights on. Students said Barclays' name could be swapped into the case.
  • Consultancy lesson: effectiveness often comes from simplification and standardisation first. Rationalise the application portfolio, standardise platforms, deal with technical debt rather than building round it, tighten delivery discipline, and put enterprise architecture and governance in place.
  • Paradox: less alignment work can make an organisation more strategically agile.
  • Right-sourcing: decide where each capability should sit; keep strategic capabilities in-house, unbundle routine work and outsource selectively without losing control. The Onfido example fits: passports and licences outsourced, complex documents (deed polls, decrees absolute) kept in-house.
  • End-to-end accountability: business and IT jointly responsible for outcomes, supported by transparency and executive oversight. A student described OKRs being used for this in Barclays UK.

Strategic alignment maturity (Luftman)

Alignment is a capability that develops; mature firms treat business and IT strategy as co-evolving. Consultants can diagnose maturity, build consensus across boundaries and turn alignment into a development path. A student noted maturity assessments are often part of a consultancy pitch.

Block Two, Lecture 5: Outsourcing and enterprise systems

Core message: outsourcing and enterprise systems are strategic choices, not operational or cost reflexes, and both succeed or fail on organisational fit. Richard taught this session online.

Opening poll: outsourcing at Barclays

  • Drivers: cost, speed of delivery, lack of internal skills, capacity and capability, and the ability to ramp up and down. A student noted MSP staff often cost more than permanent staff; the gain is flexibility.
  • Partner evaluation scored low. One team evaluated a consultancy only after it had simply rewritten their brief after four weeks; it had been chosen because other functions had used it.
  • Top risks: dependency and loss of control, closely linked, plus data security and loss of internal talent. A platform transformation team realised it was upskilling consultants on Barclays specifics instead of investing in its own staff.
  • Few clear successes. Examples given: Accenture bringing governance and structure to a three-year debit card platform migration of millions of records; an IBM payments product from about 2012 scaled into a strategic corporate banking channel; IBM coaching agile ways of working.
  • Relationships vary with the supplier manager; some partners turn collaboration into a constant sales pitch.
  • Challenges: cost, accountability, technical debt, internal red tape and non-standard builds that off-the-shelf solutions don't fit, and trust (closely tied to risk and regulation).
  • Improvements suggested: clear strategy and needs before outsourcing, scope clarity for stakeholders, logging and using lessons learned, flexible contracts, tighter evaluation.

What outsourcing is

  • The provision of IS functions by an external vendor, so the firm can focus management attention on core competencies.
  • It is not neutral. The "Kodak effect" describes how outsourcing can hollow out internal capability and lose strategic control.
  • Forms: total, selective, business process outsourcing, offshoring, and hybrids.
  • A structured decision: clarify strategic objectives and core versus non-core → assess internal capability and cost structures → evaluate external partners and the competitive landscape → only then define guidelines (financial model, supplier profile, governance, reporting).
  • A configuration, not one decision: how much to outsource, one or many vendors, who owns assets and capabilities, and over what time horizon. Design the arrangement to fit strategy; do not adopt a standard template.
  • Cloud as outsourcing: technical expectations (security, availability, latency at least equal to on-premises, with scalability and less complexity) and service expectations (efficiency, simplicity, support for innovation). It works best when those align with business strategy and governance.

DuPont case

  • Mistakes identified: over-outsourcing core IS capability, cost dominating over value creation, weak integration of IT leadership with the business, under-developed vendor management, architecture disconnected from strategy, and signals of shrinking the internal workforce.
  • Effect on alignment: IT positioned as a service provider at maturity level two to three, reduced innovation and agility, reinforced silos, and less flexibility to change sourcing later.
  • Recommendations: rebuild core IS capability (not reinsource everything), reposition IT as a strategic partner, redesign governance and vendor relationships (from transactional SLAs to risk–reward and innovation-focused contracts), align enterprise architecture with the business vision, invest in skills and career paths linked to appraisal and reward, and run ongoing maturity assessments.

Silos and enterprise systems

  • No integration: functions work on separate paper or manual flows, so data is re-created and checked at each hand-off, causing delays and errors.
  • Functional systems: each department has its own system; efficiency rises inside functions but silos are reinforced and overall visibility and coordination fall.
  • Over-computerisation: effectiveness peaks when systems fit existing processes; beyond that, adding technology without business redesign gives diminishing or negative returns. More IT does not automatically mean better outcomes.
  • Enterprise systems: one shared system and dataset across sales, warehouse and accounting; data entered once, end-to-end processes from order to invoice. The trade-off is heavy standardisation and change management. A student noted any change now affects every function, so control over changes becomes a question.
  • Three flows: information flow (decisions, status), data and document flow (orders, invoices), and physical flow (goods), synchronised by the enterprise system. Misaligned flows cause delays, errors and rework.

Organisation–enterprise system fit

Problems with enterprise systems are rarely purely technical; they are misfits between the system and the organisation (Strong and Volkoff's work on organisation–enterprise system fit). Misfits are either deficiencies (the system lacks something needed, leading to workarounds and shadow systems) or impositions (the system has the function but forces ways of working that clash with practice, culture or structure). Customisation to fix deficiencies builds technical debt.

Misfit typeWhat goes wrong
FunctionalityProcesses run through the system less efficiently; workarounds
DataPoor definitions, inconsistencies, access restrictions undermine decisions
UsabilityConfusing, cumbersome screens; lower productivity, more resistance
RoleSystem-defined roles clash with skills, authority or accountability
ControlToo much control slows work; too little exposes risk and non-compliance
Organisational cultureSystem assumptions clash with how people value and do their work

ARCO case. The enterprise system lacked support for exception-heavy work (repair, servicing, engineering), removed informal coordination, imposed finance-led data definitions, added clicks and screens, and expanded roles without support. The result was "forced alignment": practice bent round the system at the cost of performance, with staff falling back on manual and paper workarounds. Recommendations: controlled flexibility for exception-heavy areas, customise only where requirements justify it, context-sensitive data definitions with clear ownership, redesigned roles, stronger IT–business collaboration, and treating alignment as an ongoing capability.

Skills dimension and the maturity levels

  • Enterprise systems succeed when organisations invest as much in people as in technology: innovation and entrepreneurship, who holds power to initiate change, management style (command-and-control struggles with integrated systems), change readiness, hybrid staff who understand both process and IT logic, and hiring and retention in a trusting environment.
  • Luftman's five levels, which close the gap between business and IT strategy:
  1. Initial / ad hoc: reactive, fragmented, IT a cost centre, widest gap
  2. Committed process: intent to align, but practice uneven and dependent on individuals
  3. Established, focused process: defined, repeatable processes; IT embedded and leveraged
  4. Improved / managed process: effective governance and service management; IT a value centre
  5. Optimised process: business and IT strategy fully co-evolve; value extends to customers, suppliers and partners

Big-bang ERP case (final reading)

  • Issues found: a fragmented legacy environment, an IT-led SAP journey, weak governance and sponsorship, big-bang implementation, poor change management and training, and post-go-live breakdowns.
  • Alignment effects: low maturity, IT a cost centre, alignment within functions only, communication misfits, standardised processes clashing with practice, over-reliance on technical expertise.
  • Recommendations: start from business strategy, strengthen governance and senior sponsorship, phase or modularise the rollout, prioritise change management and skills, balance configuration and customisation, position IT as a partner, involve users, and plan beyond go-live.

Running a maturity assessment

  1. Set goals and form a joint business–IT team
  2. Understand the business–IT linkage and assess current maturity across the six criteria with a scoring method
  3. Analyse and prioritise gaps; differing stakeholder views are themselves useful signals
  4. Specify actions with deliverables, owners, timeframes, resources, risks and measures
  5. Choose and evaluate success criteria, reviewing them iteratively
  6. Sustain alignment as a continuous activity tied to benefits realisation

A consultant typically facilitates to keep the assessment neutral, challenge assumptions and turn gaps into a roadmap to the next level.

Conclusion: sustainable value from IT comes from disciplined management of the business–IT relationship over time, not from technology. The consultant's role is to diagnose misalignment, facilitate collaboration and guide the organisation towards it. Structure of the report

No fixed format was given, but the lecturers pointed you to three overlapping templates, and your group's notes concluded you could combine them.

  1. The consulting lifecycle as a map. Sales becomes your pitch framing; research definition and data collection draw on your literature; analysis follows; planning becomes your project plan, costs and deliverables; exit and review close it. Use the relevant phases, not all of them, and explain which mattered and which you treated lightly.
  2. The proposal structure from Lecture 2: executive summary (no costs), background, objectives and deliverables, scope and dependencies, outline approach, success measures, work plan, resources and responsibilities, cost breakdown, control and review processes, dependencies and risks, confidentiality.
  3. The final consulting report structure: executive summary, introduction (context, aim, objectives, outcomes), body, recommendations, appendices.

The brief and rubric expect, at minimum, a clearly articulated problem, project objectives, an investigation of the research area and a data collection plan.

The problem statement

  • Scope the problem and show its size and the value of solving it (Richard: weak submissions often fail to say how big the problem is or why it matters). Examples of the same problem elsewhere help.
  • Show the consultant's discipline of not accepting the client's framing. A strong scenario: the client brought you one problem, your research filtered out the noise, and you identified the real root cause.
  • Use locus of control and locus of knowledge: who raised the problem, who actually understands it, and how that shapes your pitch.
  • Describe the problem in the language of the academic literature, not your own opinion.

Aim, objectives and outcomes

  • Spend real time on these; get them wrong and the plan drifts, get them right and "the rest of the report will probably write itself".
  • Aim: one sentence, roughly 10 words, specific to the client; a statement of the question you will answer. If the group can't agree it, keep working on it. Richard will ask you to state it in a meeting.
  • Objectives: about three or four, SMART, each breaking down the aim and linked to a method, a deliverable, a time point and a cost. Test each by asking whether someone could verify at the end that you did it.
  • Outcomes: "As a result of this project the business will be able to…". Link objectives to success measures (KPIs) and say what the client cannot achieve, and at what scale, without you.
  • Both the report and presentation assess objectives, and the link from problem to aim to objectives must be tight.

Research approach and data collection plan

  • You are pitching the research journey, not the answer. Lay out: problem → research questions or hypotheses → methods → data collection → analysis → how results will be presented.
  • Choose methods only after the aim and objectives, never before ("don't grab the first bus").
  • Justify, don't declare. Write the methodology as a reasoned choice of this method over others, supported by research methods literature. A justified method is a stronger academic argument.
  • State whether the study is exploratory (inductive) or explanatory (deductive) and why. Consider triangulation to reduce bias, and mention sampling briefly if you use surveys.
  • Name a delivery methodology where relevant (Agile, hybrid, PRINCE2, a vendor method) and explain why it fits the client.
  • Draw on the methods list (documents, interviews with open, probing and closed questions, focus groups, surveys, observation, shadowing, think-aloud usability tests, value stream mapping) and analysis approaches (thematic analysis, statistics).
  • A maturity assessment against Luftman's criteria is a credible diagnostic stage for a pitch.

Literature and critical writing

  • Your expertise must come from academic literature; do not rely on your own experience as evidence. Use practitioner, vendor and web sources only for facts and statistics. Industry reports can show how you framed the work, but peer-reviewed work is the backbone.
  • You conduct a literature review but don't write one as a section. Use the literature to explain the problem and to justify the solution (for example, cite enterprise systems literature rather than saying "we think SAP fits").
  • Build each argument on several sources (synthesis). If you are citing one source at a time, you are writing descriptively.
  • Stop reading at theoretical saturation. You don't need to invent counter-arguments for a well-supported choice.
  • Where several solutions are possible, acknowledge them, then pick one and develop it, explaining that the final choice would be confirmed once on site. Lecturers do this in real KTP proposals.

Plan, costs and delivery

  • Show the work breakdown, timeline and who does what. Richard suggested the Who-does-what table helps decide team shape: strategy work is a few expensive days; integration needs more, cheaper people.
  • Costs: expect about one page. Pick a fee model (fixed fee, time and materials, risk–reward) and explain why it suits the project, including who carries the risk.
  • Design communication deliberately (progress reports, reviews, decision and issue meetings) so the client never has to chase you.
  • State what you expect from the client: data, staff time, testing, sign-off. Name dependencies, including likely silos, and treat them as risks. Keep some elements within your control; a plan that says nothing is controllable is unattractive.
  • Plan beyond go-live: handover, training, knowledge transfer, and an exit that leaves the client able to continue.
  • Build in an evaluation point and say how success will be judged; this shows the learning outcome on critical evaluation.
  • Address the duty to tell uncomfortable truths, such as process changes the client may not want to hear.

The presentation

  • Its real purpose, as in practice, is to show the client you are competent and someone they can work with; personality and delivery matter.
  • Sofiane's advice: write the report first and build the presentation from it.
  • Use the pitch as feed-forward: ask questions at the end about how to improve the report. The lecturers may not volunteer feedback unless asked.
  • Make it visual, not text-heavy. Use diagrams that map the problem, objectives and data collection plan, and include a slide showing how they interlink; talk over visuals rather than reading slides.
  • Cover the steps in order and make sure each maps to the marking criteria.

The individual reflection

  • Imagine your group's proposal has been approved and you are now conducting the project. Reflect on the ethical concerns arising from your own actions and influence on the project, team and stakeholders. You are the only unit of analysis; the ethics of the project itself is not the topic.
  • Focus on one to three ethical tensions; one handled deeply is fine. Weak submissions list many issues thinly or reflect on vague future actions.
  • Strong reflections draw on what actually happened in your group's pitch: what you emphasised because the client wanted it, what you left out (for example, ROI over the impact on staff), and team dynamics.
  • Underpin with academic literature: about three or four references. "Theory" means literature that supports your point; consequentialist and non-consequentialist ethics will be introduced in Block Three, and motivational theories could also fit.
  • No full reflective model is needed (Gibbs won't fit in 1,000 words). Answer what the issue is, why it is a problem, how it is an ethical tension, and how you feel about it.
  • Ideas raised in class: the duty to speak uncomfortable truths; solving the stated problem while masking the real one; access to more information than the brief needs during a holistic review; upselling and follow-on work; vendor bias in recommendations; existing relationships and loyalties as an insider (an advantage and a complication); knowledge transfer and client dependency; regulatory tensions, which Richard said often yield good reflections.
